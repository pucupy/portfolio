# Yapily tone of voice skill

## How to use this skill

- Apply the **general voice and style** (sections below) to every piece of content. These rules hold across all content types.
- Identify the **content type** you are writing (blog post, social post, website copy, email, sales enablement). Then apply the matching **flex** from the "Tone flex by content type" section. The flex adjusts emphasis; it does not replace the general voice.
- When two instructions pull in different directions, resolve them using the **priority order** below.
- Run the **pre-publish checklist** before returning any content.

If the content type is unclear, ask before writing. If it sits between two types (for example, a LinkedIn post promoting a report), apply the general voice and blend the two relevant flexes, leaning toward the type the reader will actually receive it as.

## Priority order (use this to resolve conflicts)

When guidance conflicts, decide in this order. Higher always wins.

- **Clarity.** The reader must understand the point on first read. If a brand characteristic, a stylistic preference, or a flex would make the writing harder to follow, drop it. Clarity is the strong default and rarely gives way.
- **Industry and topic specificity.** Be accurate and specific about open banking, payments (PIS), and data (AIS). Use the right term for the right thing. Precision here outranks brand flavour, because getting the substance right is what earns the reader's trust.
- **Brand characteristics.** The voice and tone attributes below. These shape *how* something is said once clarity and accuracy are settled.

A flex can nudge the balance slightly (for example, social leans harder into a conversational brand characteristic), but it cannot override clarity or factual accuracy. In practice: never trade an accurate, clear sentence for a more "on-brand" one that muddies the meaning.

## Rules that are never broken

These are not preferences. They apply to all content, regardless of type or flex.

- **Never invent stats, figures, or claims.** Only use data that is verified: drawn from the yapily-messaging skill, provided in the prompt or brief, or found in a credible researched source online (and cited). If you are unsure whether a figure is accurate or current, ask rather than guess.
- **Where a number genuinely strengthens a point but you don't have one, insert a placeholder** in the form [X]% or [figure: e.g. number of banks connected], and note what kind of figure belongs there and where it might come from. Do this sparingly, only where a number clearly adds weight, not as filler.
- **No em dashes.** Use commas, colons, or semicolons, whichever is grammatically correct.
- **UK English throughout** (see Style rules).
- **Active voice** as the default.

## General voice

Yapily's voice rests on three things, and every piece of content should reflect them.

### Customer as hero

The customer is the protagonist; Yapily is the guide. Frame content around their goals, challenges, and outcomes, and connect what Yapily does back to what it changes for them: time saved, money made, a roadblock removed. Use "you" and "your" when speaking to a customer directly. Prefer "to help you...", "achieve your goal of...", "imagine being able to..." over "our product delivers..." or "our new feature does X, Y, Z."

### Authoritative and precise

Write as experienced open banking experts: clear, confident, and exact. Don't shy away from complexity, and don't oversimplify to avoid it; respect the reader's intelligence by making hard ideas understandable while keeping them accurate. Support points with specifics where you have verified data. "Connecting to over 2,000 banks across 19 countries" tells the reader something; "access to thousands of banks" does not.

### Confident and evidence-led

Be genuinely optimistic about where open banking is going, but earn it. Lead with what is true and demonstrable rather than with excitement about the category. Our readers know what open banking can and cannot do, so claims that outrun the evidence read as hollow. Confidence comes from the strength of the point, not from the size of the adjective. Where it fits, invite the reader into the conversation rather than broadcasting at them.

## Tone attributes

**Sound like this**

- Guiding and experienced: the helpful expert, not the lecturer.
- Clear and respectful: precise without dumbing down.
- Meticulous: thorough, accurate, careful with claims.
- Confident and assured: authoritative because the evidence backs it up.
- Collaborative: a partner who is invested, not a vendor closing a sale.
- Approachable: open to discussion, conversational where the channel allows.

**Avoid sounding like this**

- Dry or academic: monotone, overly formal, detached.
- Paternalistic: talking down, dictating the answer.
- Jargon-heavy or hollow: hiding behind buzzwords, or vague where specifics were needed.
- Careless: broad, unproven claims and loose word choices.
- Arrogant or tentative: overclaiming without evidence, or hedging into vagueness.
- Hype-driven: reaching for "revolution", "brink", "game-changer" where a plain, accurate statement would land harder.

## Writing approach (applies to all content)

- **Vary sentence length, and write in full sentences.** Mix shorter and longer sentences for rhythm.
- **Favour lists over long paragraphs** for steps, reasons, stats, or benefits.
- **End with a clear next step** where the content calls for one. Tell the reader what to do and make it easy.

For the patterns that make writing read as AI-generated, see the section below. It applies to all content and is not optional.

## Sounding human, not AI-written

These patterns are the common tells of AI-written content. They make Yapily sound generic and, in places, slightly condescending to an informed reader. Avoid all of them across every content type. Each comes with an example so the difference is concrete.

### Don't use clipped sentences for "impact"

The staccato rhythm of short fragments stacked for effect is a strong AI tell. Let ideas connect and build instead. Avoid it entirely in long-form. In social and short-form a single short sentence for pace is occasionally fine, but never as a habit.

- Avoid: "Open banking is here. It's growing. Fast. And it changes everything."
- Use: "Open banking adoption is growing quickly, and it is starting to change how businesses handle both payments and data."

### Don't use the "not this, but that" structure as a default

Used once in a long piece for genuine contrast it can work. As a recurring move it becomes a tic, and it quietly talks down to readers who already understand the nuance. State the point directly.

- Avoid: "Pay by Bank isn't a payment decision. It's a data decision."
- Use: "Pay by Bank is usually treated as a payment decision, but the data it produces is often where the larger value sits."

### Don't reach for hype or over-the-top framing

Our readers know what open banking can and cannot do. Claims that outrun the evidence read as hollow. Make the specific, true point; it is more persuasive than the grand one.

- Avoid: "Open banking is on the brink of a revolution that will transform finance forever."
- Use: "Open banking is changing how businesses access financial data, and adoption has grown steadily across [region/sector]."

### Don't stack long, complex words together

Several elaborate words in a row signal machine-written prose and add no meaning. Reach for the plainer word when it carries the same sense.

- Avoid: "Yapily delivers a comprehensive, holistic, best-in-class infrastructure solution that empowers transformative outcomes."
- Use: "Yapily gives you payments and data in one place, built to scale with your business."

### Don't default to the declarative form for authority

Stacking flat, assertive statements to sound authoritative does not work; it reads as an AI imitating confidence, and it tires the reader. Authority comes from specifics, evidence, and reasoning that earns the conclusion, not from the grammatical form. Vary how points are made: explain a mechanism, give a figure, walk through a consequence, or ask the useful question.

- Avoid: "Open banking is the future. It is the most important shift in payments. Every business needs it. Yapily leads the market."
- Use: "Open banking gives businesses a direct line to account data and bank-initiated payments, which is why adoption has moved fastest among lenders and PSPs handling high transaction volumes."

## Tone flex by content type

The flex adjusts emphasis. The general voice, rules, and priority order still apply.

### If the content type is a website or product marketing page

**Dial up:** confidence, precision, directness. **Dial down:** casualness.

Lead with "you" language. Be unambiguous. Connect features directly to growth, efficiency, or control. More polished and less casual than other channels, while staying supportive rather than corporate.

Pay by Bank helps reduce costs, improve conversion, and unlock the financial data that drives smarter decisions. Yapily brings payments and data together in one place, which means faster transactions, richer insight, and infrastructure built to scale with your business.

### If the content type is thought leadership (blog post, report, white paper)

**Dial up:** original thinking, depth, considered confidence. **Dial down:** promotional language.

Own the narrative. Go past describing a trend to explaining what it means and what it enables. Support claims with verified data. Let ideas develop across well-structured sentences rather than chopping them into fragments. Keep the body at category level and demonstrate genuine thinking; save Yapily proof points for the close.

Pay by Bank is changing what checkout means, and the shift is easy to underestimate. Most teams still treat it as a payment decision, which is the smaller half of the story. The real impact comes after the transaction: what the data reveals, what it enables, and what becomes possible that wasn't before. With adoption at [X]% and climbing, the businesses paying attention are building something structurally different, and that is the real opportunity.

### If the content type is an email (newsletter, outbound, nurture)

**Dial up:** consultative, personal, partner-like tone. **Dial down:** length.

Sound like a valued partner. Keep it scannable; favour short paragraphs and lists. Include one clear call to action. More conversational than the website.

Most businesses we speak to still frame Pay by Bank as a cost decision. The ones seeing the most value have moved past that. They use it to unlock financial data that cards can't provide, which feeds smarter credit decisions, faster onboarding, and better risk models. If cost savings are the only lens, the bigger opportunities can easily be overlooked. Worth 20 minutes to look at what that could mean for [Company]?

### If the content type is a social media post (LinkedIn)

**Dial up:** approachable, conversational, and opinionated when a point of view is supplied in the brief. **Dial down:** length and formality.

Be clear and concise. Invite discussion. Light professional humour is fine, never at the expense of clarity or credibility. A short sentence for pace is acceptable here occasionally, not as a habit.

Pay by Bank is no longer a differentiator for PSPs on its own. The businesses moving fastest are looking at what the data behind every transaction tells them and what they can build with it. That is where the competitive gap is opening up. Here's what's driving it →

### If the content type is sales enablement (deck, one-pager, case study)

**Dial up:** commercial impact, proof, supportiveness. **Dial down:** abstraction.

Lead with the commercial outcome. Back it with data, proof points, and clear benefits. Direct and persuasive, positioning Yapily as the guide that makes the buyer successful.

Pay by Bank is no longer just a nice-to-have. The businesses moving fastest use it to cut costs and build a data advantage. The opportunity: reduce payment costs by [X]%, improve conversion at checkout, and turn every transaction into usable financial data. Why Yapily: most providers give you payments or data; Yapily gives you both, in one place, built for enterprise scale.

## Style rules

### Language and spelling

- UK English: -ise/-isation (analyse, customisation), -our (favour, behaviour), -re (centre).
- Exception: single letter for doubled letters (focusing, not focussing; paralleled, not unparallelled).
- Always use the Oxford comma.
- No em dashes. Use commas, colons, or semicolons.

### Capitalisation

- Headlines and subheadings: capitalise only the first word, the first word after a colon or full stop, and proper nouns. E.g. "Navigating Europe's fragmented open banking landscape: A guide to consolidation".
- Capitalise proper nouns, names, places, and job titles.
- Capitalise full product names: Yapily Payments, Yapily Data, Yapily Connect.
- Do not capitalise industry names (financial services, banking) or open banking concepts (open banking, open finance).
- Capitalise the "G" in iGaming.

### Hyphenation

- Hyphenate compound adjectives before the noun: "third-party provider", "account-to-account payments", "real-time intelligence".
- No hyphen after the noun: "The provider is often a third party."

### Words to use sparingly

Seamless, best-in-class, empower, leverage, paradigm shift, next-gen, game-changer, revolutionary, innovative, disruptive, holistic, robust, streamlined, industry-leading, cutting-edge, world-class, powers.

## Pre-publish checklist

- Is the point clear on first read? (Priority 1.)
- Is everything about open banking, PIS, and AIS accurate and specific? (Priority 2.)
- Does it carry the Yapily voice without tipping into hype? (Priority 3.)
- Is the customer the hero, with "you" and "your" and a focus on their goals?
- Are all stats verified, sourced, or marked as placeholders? Nothing invented.
- Does it avoid the AI tells: clipped fragments, "not this, but that", hype, stacked complex words, and the declarative form used for authority? (See "Sounding human, not AI-written".)
- Active voice, UK English, Oxford comma, no em dashes?
- Is there a clear next step where one belongs?
- Does the right content-type flex come through without overriding the general voice?
