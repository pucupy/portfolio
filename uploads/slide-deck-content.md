# Instructions for Claude Design

## Overall presentation style

- Editorial, confident and evidence-led.
- Dark or near-black background with restrained use of the existing portfolio accent.
- Large typography, generous spacing and minimal decoration.
- Prefer real artefacts, diagrams, research outputs, prototypes and product screens over stock imagery.
- Use one clear message per slide.
- Keep on-slide text short. Put context, caveats and detailed evidence in speaker notes.
- Make metrics visually prominent but always retain attribution and confidentiality caveats.
- Show progression: **Understand → Define → Architect → Explore → Validate → Build → Launch → Optimise**.
- Avoid presenting design as a linear process when the work was iterative. Use the framework as a narrative, not as a claim that every phase happened once.

## Reusable slide anatomy

Each slide may contain:

- `TITLE`
- `ON-SLIDE COPY`
- `VISUAL DIRECTION`
- `SPEAKER NOTES`
- `SOURCE / ASSET`

## Public-content rules

- Hosted Pages is still in beta as of August 2026. Do not describe it as generally available.
- Hosted Pages conversion and integration figures are public; per-feature conversion movements should be described as directional unless exact numbers are later confirmed.
- Do not publish exact Console ticket, compliance-queue, setup-time or backlog counts.
- Do not publish Brand & Website pipeline or enquiry metrics.
- Do not claim ownership of HeadBox’s matching or AI models.
- Use £150,228 ARR for HeadBox, not £150,288.
- Use 10% for the original HeadBox marketplace conversion.
- It is accurate to say 90% of users left before sending an enquiry.
- The current HeadBox flow remains approximately 90% structurally the same.

# Deck 01 — Yapily Hosted Pages Deep Dive

**Recommended length:** 18–22 slides / 35–45 minutes  
**Purpose:** Product design interview, fintech or platform role, detailed case-study discussion.

---

## Slide 1 — Case-study title

### TITLE
Yapily Hosted Pages

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Making open banking easier to launch, use and trust

### SECONDARY
Lead Product Designer & Design Manager · 2022–present · Live in beta

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Hero product image showing a customer checkout transitioning into bank selection.

---

## Slide 2 — Outcome first

### TITLE
From months of custom work to a launch path of around two weeks

### ON-SLIDE COPY
- Integration: approximately 4–6 months in many European cases → around 2 weeks
- Conversion: roughly 20% in early beta → approximately 65–75% today
- UK app-to-app journeys can exceed 85%

### SPEAKER NOTES
The integration figure was observed by Pre-sales and Implementation through the reduced need for guidance and support. Conversion varies by use case, market, bank, device and infrastructure reliability. Design contributed, but frontend, backend, coverage and bank reliability were equally important.

---

## Slide 3 — The original problem

### TITLE
The API gave customers capability, not a usable journey

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Customers still had to build:
- Checkout and consent
- Bank discovery
- Authentication
- Error and retry states
- Market and bank-specific constraints

### SPEAKER NOTES
I interviewed payment companies, identity-verification companies and banks. Participants included CTOs, CPOs, Heads of Product, Design and Technology, and lead engineers. The recurring issue was not a missing screen. Customers were being forced to become experts in hundreds of banks and regional rules.

---

## Slide 4 — Why documentation was not enough

### TITLE
We first tried to explain the complexity

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Better documentation helped. It did not remove the work.

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Two-column comparison: “Document the plumbing” vs “Remove the plumbing”.

### SPEAKER NOTES
The first response was improved documentation and guidance. Customers still spent months integrating, testing banks and maintaining exceptions. Some smaller companies churned before launch. Since Yapily’s commercial value depends on successful consents after launch, slow integration was a product and business problem.

---

## Slide 5 — Who the product was for

### TITLE
The majority needed a faster path

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Approximately 80% of customers were smaller or non-enterprise businesses

### SPEAKER NOTES
The first target was SMEs, which represented the largest group and needed the least support-heavy route. Enterprises also used Hosted Pages for quick tests, account verification or markets where building a bespoke journey was not economically worthwhile.

---

## Slide 6 — The product bet

### TITLE
Turn repeatable integration work into product capability

### ON-SLIDE COPY
A compliant, white-label flow for payments and account data

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Show a simple chain: Merchant → Hosted Pages → Bank → Merchant.

### SPEAKER NOTES
The earliest concepts emerged in a 2022 Product and Technology brainstorm. We considered SDKs and widgets. The first version prioritised a practical hosted experience that could launch quickly. Broader embeddability, SDKs, returning-user support and some authentication patterns were left out of the earliest scope.

---

## Slide 7 — My role and the team

### TITLE
Strategy, design and continuing ownership

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Initial team: PM + frontend engineer + me  
Later: design, frontend, backend and analytics support

### SPEAKER NOTES
I led the initial product strategy and design, created the reference wireflows, worked with Compliance and Engineering, and tested the experience. As the team grew, I continued to own direction, customer conversations, workshops and design quality while delegating more detailed testing and design-system work.

---

## Slide 8 — The visible flow hides a matrix

### TITLE
Five screens. More than forty possible journeys.

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Licence × Product × Market × Device × Bank capability

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Create a branching matrix. Highlight that some combinations do not exist.

### SPEAKER NOTES
The customer’s licence model and API configuration determine some branches. Market, device, product and bank capability determine others. A preselected institution may skip bank selection. A direct-licence customer may remove a consent screen. cVRP exists only in the UK. This is a sparse matrix, not a neat grid.

## Slide 9 — Authentication multiplies it again

### TITLE
The journey stops being ours

### ON-SLIDE COPY
- Browser redirect
- Embedded login
- Desktop-to-mobile handoff
- Direct handoff after bank selection

### SPEAKER NOTES
Which model a user receives depends on their bank and country. The bank experience itself is outside Yapily’s control, but Hosted Pages must prepare the user, make the handoff understandable and recover correctly when control returns.

---

## Slide 10 — Architecting the system

### TITLE
Reference wireflows became backend logic

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Design + Product + Business Analysis + Engineering + market expertise

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Use flow artefact if available. Show a simplified system map with design inputs and runtime decisions.

### SPEAKER NOTES
I worked with business analysts, PMs and engineers to map payments and data journeys. Bank capability data and market knowledge were translated into conditions the backend could execute. The key design artefacts were reference wireflows, funnel definitions and journey maps rather than isolated screens.

---

## Slide 11 — Early bank selection

### TITLE
A familiar layout built on unreliable assumptions

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Hard-coded popular bank grid  
Wrong names · weak aliases · repeated logos · poor scale

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Use `choose bank.png` or before/after mockups.

### SPEAKER NOTES
The grid was visually prominent but difficult to maintain. Labels did not fit, branch-heavy countries produced repetitive results and search did not reflect how people referred to their bank. We explored grids, two columns, search-only and accordions.

---

## Slide 12 — Research model

### TITLE
Match the method to the decision

### ON-SLIDE COPY
- Moderated: reasoning and expectations
- Unmoderated: coverage, speed and error rates
- Production experiments: real traffic and real banks

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Use `interview.jpg`, `interview2.jpg` or `learnings.jpg`.

### SPEAKER NOTES
Early rounds were often 10–15 users in a market. Larger unmoderated studies covered hundreds of people across around 20 banks. Qualitative work discarded weak ideas quickly. Stronger concepts moved into percentage rollouts and live experiments.

---

## Slide 13 — Finding the bank

### TITLE
What looked like a layout problem was an intelligence problem

### ON-SLIDE COPY
- Popular banks
- Recent bank
- Regional grouping
- Better display names
- Aliases and synonyms
- Accent-insensitive search

### SPEAKER NOTES
Search was consistently the main way people found their bank and could take five to fifteen seconds. The winning direction was a single-column list supported by better naming and search data. Popular-bank testing brought lookup below three seconds in many cases. Later measurements showed fewer people unable to find a bank, a small rise in successful selection and lower search use.

---

## Slide 14 — Returning users

### TITLE
Two optimisations wanted the same space

### ON-SLIDE COPY
First-time users need popular banks  
Returning users need their previous bank

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Show first visit and repeat visit states side by side.

### SPEAKER NOTES
Rather than create two competing sections, the recent bank was promoted into the first slot and popular banks filled the remaining positions. De-duplication prevented the same institution appearing twice.

---

## Slide 15 — The QR assumption

### TITLE
Sometimes the best QR screen is no QR screen

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Remove it where app-to-app coverage is weak  
Improve it where the handoff genuinely helps

### SPEAKER NOTES
The original assumption was that mobile bank apps were more usable, so desktop journeys should hand off to a phone. Outside the UK, coverage was inconsistent. Some banks introduced their own QR codes and users faced competing codes. In weak markets we removed the Yapily QR step. In stronger markets we improved the guidance and layout.

---

## Slide 16 — Embedded authentication

### TITLE
The screen has to teach users to distrust screens like it

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Accurate fields · named bank · regulated provider · customer branding · explicit warning

### SPEAKER NOTES
Entering bank credentials into an unfamiliar interface resembles phishing. Generic padlock language was not enough. Testing improved bank-specific field mapping and trust signals. The experience explicitly warns users never to enter a one-time code where it should only be used inside their bank’s own experience.

---

## Slide 17 — Research found backend problems

### TITLE
Design did not always own the fix

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Retry limits were different by bank

### SPEAKER NOTES
The interface assumed generic retry behaviour. Some banks allowed more credential or SCA attempts than Hosted Pages. Research surfaced the mismatch; Engineering created per-bank mappings. This is a recurring pattern in the case study: research found the user problem, but the solution belonged in platform intelligence.

---

## Slide 18 — Accessibility as a release gate

### TITLE
We delayed the release by two sprints

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Known barriers were fixed before launch

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Use `designsystem.jpg` or an accessibility audit snippet.

### SPEAKER NOTES
An external study identified contrast failures and screen readers announcing raw SVG markup. We postponed the release to address both. The target was WCAG 2.1 AA, and the findings influenced components and code rather than only visual design.

---

## Slide 19 — Custard design system

### TITLE
White-label should mean controlled flexibility

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Customer logo + colour  
Stable interaction, accessibility and compliance

### SPEAKER NOTES
Hosted Pages needed its own system because customers theme the surface. Custard exposes a limited, deliberate set of brand controls while locking the elements that protect usability and compliance. It shares foundations where useful but keeps product-specific components separate.

---

## Slide 20 — Build and rollout

### TITLE
Launch in the order the market demanded

### ON-SLIDE COPY
UK payments → data → Germany → wider Europe

### SPEAKER NOTES
Prototypes began in late 2022 and early 2023. Alpha and beta work followed, with the first launch around May 2023. Rollout was shaped by the customer pipeline. Reliability was the main launch risk because visual quality cannot compensate for an unsuccessful bank connection.

---

## Slide 21 — Support ecosystem

### TITLE
Self-service for most. Guidance for customers who need control.

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Hosted Pages  
+ UX guidance  
+ interactive demos

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Use `hub.png`; show links as product support ecosystem.

### SPEAKER NOTES
Enterprise and direct-licence customers still needed help designing their own checkouts, compliance screens, bank lists, authentication and consent dashboards. I worked with Pre-sales and Implementation, designed the documentation and built the first interactive demos. My team continued expanding them.

---

## Slide 22 — Apple demo

### TITLE
Make the platform feel real

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Realism + storytelling

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Add Apple-inspired demo screenshots and live link when shared.

### SPEAKER NOTES
The demos helped Sales, Pre-sales, Implementation and customers understand how payments or data sharing could fit into a recognisable product. The Apple-inspired version is the strongest example because it makes the open-banking flow feel like part of a real customer experience rather than an abstract technical demo.

---

## Slide 23 — Results

### TITLE
A long sequence of small, evidenced improvements

### ON-SLIDE COPY
~2 weeks integration  
~65–75% end-to-end conversion  
Faster and more reliable bank discovery

### SPEAKER NOTES
Do not attribute the entire conversion movement to design. Bank coverage, API reliability, frontend and backend work all contributed. Design’s clearest contribution was in discoverability, handoff guidance, authentication accuracy, trust and accessibility.

---

## Slide 24 — Reflection

### TITLE
I would design for licence flexibility and embeddability earlier

### ON-SLIDE COPY
One product can hide more than one customer type

### SPEAKER NOTES
The first version was appropriately focused on customers using Yapily’s licence. Direct-licence customers later needed more flexibility. The lesson is not to anticipate everything; it is to identify when one surface serves genuinely different customer models before the retrofit becomes expensive.

Yapily Console

Turning a sales-led onboarding journey into a self-service platform

Lead Product Designer & Design Manager · 2020–present · New Console in beta

---

## Slide 2 — Outcome first

### TITLE
The goal was not a better dashboard

### ON-SLIDE COPY
The goal was to remove repeatable human work from the customer journey

### SPEAKER NOTES
Customers could not onboard, enable products, configure Hosted Pages or manage compliance without employees acting on their behalf. The new strategy separates enterprise customers who need support from the majority who should be able to self-serve.

---

## Slide 3 — What the original product was

### TITLE
A certificate vault for five customers

### ON-SLIDE COPY
2020 · One licence model · One application container

### SPEAKER NOTES
The Dashboard was built for the small number of customers that held their own open banking licence. It was appropriate for that moment but had almost no product analytics, organisational model or onboarding guidance.

---

## Slide 4 — The company changed around it

### TITLE
The API moved. The Console did not.

### ON-SLIDE COPY
New markets · new licence model · new products · thousands of banks

### SPEAKER NOTES
Between 2022 and 2025, Yapily migrated major technical foundations and repeatedly rebranded the Console without substantially extending the experience. Legacy Vue packages, a Java backend and missing organisation concepts made changes expensive.

---

## Slide 5 — The visible symptom

### TITLE
Every missing feature became somebody’s job

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Support · Customer Success · Compliance · Product · Engineering

### SPEAKER NOTES
Employees enabled products, curated institution coverage, managed customisation, explained compliance and moved customers through onboarding. The product gap presented as a need for more headcount.

---

## Slide 6 — The customer impact

### TITLE
Six to twelve months to production

### ON-SLIDE COPY
The journey moved at the speed of whoever had calendar space

### SPEAKER NOTES
Pre-sales, implementation and customer-success reports showed onboarding could take six months or more. Enterprise customers expected support. Smaller customers, roughly 80% of the base, were sitting through a process built for much larger accounts.

---

## Slide 7 — Make the cost visible

### TITLE
A design argument was not enough

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Customer interviews + cross-functional workshop + two-day hackathon

### SPEAKER NOTES
Earlier attempts had failed because Design raised the problem only with Product. This time Sales, Customer Success, Support, Engineering, Product and Design built a shared account of the journey.

---

## Slide 8 — The hackathon

### TITLE
Use the product like an outsider

### ON-SLIDE COPY
No internal shortcuts

### SPEAKER NOTES
Engineering, PMs, Design and Implementation role-played as a customer. They encountered permissions failures, sandbox problems, confusing IDs and an undocumented CORS issue. Two issues were fixed directly: the CORS problem and a more complete sandbox that also improved automated testing.

---

## Slide 9 — Journey map

### TITLE
The interface, commercial process and operating model were one experience

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Evaluate → contract → compliance → implement → go live → renew

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Use a service blueprint with channels, teams, manual steps and dead ends.

### SPEAKER NOTES
I mapped the full journey rather than only the logged-in experience. It included website, documentation, support, sales, compliance, technical onboarding and renewal. The map made visible how many steps were manual and how little continuity existed between channels.

---

## Slide 10 — Three findings

### TITLE
The problem had a clear shape

### ON-SLIDE COPY
1. Nobody could get live without us  
2. Everyone received the same journey  
3. Users were invisible across channels

### SPEAKER NOTES
The most important finding was not one broken step. The whole system depended on employee availability. Enterprise and smaller customers received the same path despite having different expectations and value.

---

## Slide 11 — Strategic decision

### TITLE
Split the path

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Enterprise: assisted  
Most customers: self-service

### VISUAL DIRECTION
Two-path diagram, converging only where shared infrastructure is useful.

### SPEAKER NOTES
The design did not try to eliminate human support. It reserved it for customers whose complexity warrants it and moved repeatable work into the product for everyone else.

---

## Slide 12 — Target journey

### TITLE
Account to live in one to two months for existing products

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Minimal assistance · transparent steps · clear status

### SPEAKER NOTES
This is the target outcome, not yet a fully measured adoption result. The new Console is in beta and the remaining rollout risk is mainly around single-tenant environments and technical testing.

---

## Slide 13 — Information architecture

### TITLE
Organisation became the commercial container

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Organisation  
Users & Access  
Applications  
Institutions  
Settings

### SPEAKER NOTES
The old model placed too much inside an application. The new architecture separates company-level concerns—billing, entitlements, teams, jurisdictions and audit—from technical application configuration.

---

## Slide 14 — Application and sub-applications

### TITLE
One customer is not always one company

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Merchants · regions · compliance states

### SPEAKER NOTES
Sub-applications support both reseller merchants and regional entities. Each can hold its own compliance status, so one may be live while another remains under review. This solved both operational and billing problems created by the original one-application model.

---

## Slide 15 — Guided onboarding

### TITLE
Make the next step self-evident

### ON-SLIDE COPY
A seven-step route instead of a Customer Success explanation

### SPEAKER NOTES
Testing showed the guided journey reduced ambiguity and the need for external instruction. The flow covers account, organisation, compliance, application setup, product scope and first successful implementation.

## Slide 16 — Analytics

### TITLE

### ON-SLIDE COPY

### SPEAKER NOTES
Analytics was initially delayed but became essential. Customers need to separate product drop-off from bank reliability, identify specific failed sessions and understand where conversion changes across markets, devices and institutions.

### ON-SLIDE COPY
Product · Engineering · Compliance · Finance · Design · Support · CSM

# Appendix E — Presenter Q&A Notes

## Hosted Pages: questions to expect

### Why not build SDKs first?
SDKs were considered, but the initial hosted approach offered a faster route to market across multiple banks and compliance states. SDK complexity and embeddability were deferred. Starting again, I would design the architecture to support embeddability earlier.

### How much of the conversion improvement was design?
It cannot be cleanly separated. UX improved bank discoverability, trust, guidance and accuracy. Frontend, backend, coverage and bank reliability were major contributors. The public claim should present the movement as a cross-functional outcome.

### Why did the product remain in beta?
It continued to expand across markets, customer types and integration modes. As of August 2026 it was still in beta and should not be described as generally available.

### How did Compliance influence the experience?
Compliance reviewed regulated content and licence requirements. Design worked with Compliance during product development rather than at the end. The objective was to make the necessary information understandable without turning the journey into an intimidating page of legal text.

### How did you decide what to ship?
A direction shipped when the primary metric showed a reliable positive movement and guardrails remained stable. Mixed evidence led to iteration. No effect, negative impact or changed context led to stopping.

---

## Console: questions to expect

### Why was the Console deprioritised for so long?
Platform expansion and technical migration were considered more directly tied to growth. The turning point came when multiple functions made the operational cost visible together rather than Design presenting it as an interface problem.

### What exactly did you own?
The end-to-end journey map and information architecture were mine. Product strategy and self-service scope were defined jointly with Product and Engineering. Detailed design was shared across the team, with me remaining hands-on in concepts, research, workshops, review and quality.

### What has actually shipped?
The new Console is in beta. Hosted Pages configuration, sub-applications, webhooks and other pieces shipped earlier. The public story should distinguish legacy, beta and future-state screens.

### Why not publish the ticket reductions?
The detailed support, compliance and setup figures are confidential. The mechanism and directional movement are safe to discuss.

---

## HeadBox: questions to expect

### Was the original marketplace conversion 1% or 10%?
Use 10%. It is also accurate that 90% of users left before sending an enquiry.

### Did the new model remove commission?
Use the latest confirmed description: venues paid a subscription for access to Lead Feed, with commission retained on confirmed bookings.

### How much of the current product is yours?
The current Lead Feed and guest journey remain approximately 90% structurally the same, with small refinements. It is valid to show the current interface while stating that the original design has evolved through later teams.

### Did you build the matching AI?
No. Analytics and later models improved matching, but I was not involved in creating the models and should not claim ownership.

### What would you change?
Very little about the product strategy. The corporate path was included early, the experience supported both low- and high-volume venues, and the original instrumentation raised no significant concerns.

They completed the final visual designs. I provided creative direction, first concepts and wireframes, managed the relationship, collaborated with frontend on implementation and governed the component system and wider brand rollout.
